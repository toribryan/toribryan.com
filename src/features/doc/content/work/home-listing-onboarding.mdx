## The problem

Modern Care Homes lists residential assisted living homes in Arizona. Every
one of them already holds a state license, so the owner and the address are
on record before the owner ever visits the site.

The first version used that, but only from the admin side. An admin invited
the owner from their license, and the owner landed in the full Manage Home
editor with a checklist of everything a listing needs. Nothing would save
without three gallery photos, and the publish button put the home live right
away.

## The design solution

<Numbered>
  <Item title="Start from the license.">
    Owners search for their own home. Picking it fills in the name, address,
    care type and capacity from the state record, so the first question is just
    "is this you?" Progress is underway before they type anything, which is the
    whole point of momentum.
  </Item>
  <Item title="One topic per screen, saved as they go.">
    Seven short steps in three phases, each saved on its own. Photos and care
    are no longer required for a draft, so an owner can stop at any point and
    pick up later. Progressive disclosure does the rest: each screen asks only
    what its step needs.
  </Item>
  <Item title="Draw the home as it's described.">
    Every step has an isometric illustration that reacts to the form. Long
    checklists turn into a yard that fills up, which shows progress better than
    a counter does. It borrows the reward of a game's progress loop without the
    points and badges.
  </Item>
  <Item title="Review before families see it.">
    The last step submits the home to the Modern Care team instead of publishing
    it, and emails them a link to check it. That step is friction on purpose,
    the one place a slower path protects families.
  </Item>
  <Item title="Readable for everyone.">
    Welcome text stays at 16px or larger, the illustration pins above the form
    on phones, and on touch screens a tap never moves the page.
  </Item>
</Numbered>

## How it got here

The illustrations started as a way to make a long form feel shorter, and
changed a lot once I could click through them.

### The yard started as an empty lot

The first amenities stage was a blank grid of plots waiting to be filled.
The next version filled them, but in black outlines with flat color, and the
objects floated with no sense of a place. The shipped yard drops the heavy
outlines, gives each object its own detail and an animation when it lands,
and arranges them like a real backyard.

## An illustration system

I wanted setting up a listing to feel delightful, not like paperwork. The
illustrations carry that: each one moves as the owner answers, so every
checkbox and every room gets a small reward on screen.

The figures are one isometric system, drawn as SVG components in the
codebase and animated in CSS, so each one reads the form's state directly.
Nothing is a static export.

### From a skill to a brand

I started the figures with
[iso-figure](https://github.com/MrBongoC/ai-iso-skill), a skill that has
Claude draw isometric line art as plain SVG. It gave me a base where the
angles, line weights and proportions already agreed. From there I designed
on top of it in Figma to make it friendly and on brand: fuller color from
Modern Care's palette, softer shapes, and a yard with room for every amenity
an owner can pick.

## After it's live

Once the team approves a home, the owner keeps it current in the Manage
dashboard, the editor new owners used to land in on day one. Spencer built
the dashboard; I redesigned the two pages owners come back to most, so they
read like the flow that came before them.

### Care, in the same eight groups

The care step and the dashboard share one taxonomy. Care is grouped by the
same eight axes, and in the dashboard each axis opens in its own dialog, a
bottom drawer on a phone. Saving one axis replaces only that axis's
answers, so two people editing different parts of a home don't overwrite
each other.

### Availability families can trust

Open rooms are what families act on, so the Availability page leads with
how fresh they are. A badge reads when the owner last confirmed them, rooms
sit in one table with their details as chips, and several can be updated or
removed at once. With no rooms, the page shows an example room and says
plainly that the home stays unavailable until one is added.

## How I built it

I designed this in code rather than in Figma. The flow and its illustrations
were new, and the questions that mattered, like how a yard fills up, how a
camera pans and how a step feels on a phone, only had answers once they moved.

- **Storybook first.** Every step has stories at desktop and phone widths, including the awkward states: an empty price, a shared room with no gender set, a review with something missing.
- **Smooth motion.** The care camera panned at 23fps. Moving the transform onto the SVG's own box brought it to 60.
- **Built on the shared system.** The steps use the product's design system components, with one new pattern: a double-bordered panel that frames every block of content in the flow.
- **Tested where it counts.** Submitting is covered for an incomplete draft, a non-owner and a home that's already been submitted.

## Where it stands

The flow is complete and running behind the `home-onboarding` feature flag
while the pull request is in review. Before it opens to owners I want to
check touch behavior on a real phone, and then measure time to submit and
where owners stop, step by step. The next piece I'd design is the email that
says a home is live: it should show owners their listing as families see it,
so coming back feels like picking up where they left off.
