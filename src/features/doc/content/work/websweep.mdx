## The institution is the last to know

Assessment content is expensive to write and easy to lose. A single question
photographed and posted to a homework-help site is enough to devalue an exam
that took months to build, and the institution is usually the last to know.

WebSweep scans the web for leaked assessment content and surfaces what it finds.
From there the point isn't the alert, it's the next move: send a takedown
notice, or swap the affected questions and keep the exam usable.

## A find is only useful if it comes with a next step

The product has two jobs in its navigation, Scan and Takedown, and the whole
dashboard is arranged so a find never dead-ends.

- **Resolution rate is the headline number**, not the count of leaks. The
  figure an administrator sees first is how much compromised content has
  already been removed from the internet, because that is the number they are
  accountable for.
- **Activity over time** shows leaks found against leaks resolved, so a spike
  reads as a trend to act on rather than a one-off alarm.
- **Compromised content is a working table**, not a report: every row is a
  course and quiz with the leaked content and its link count, filterable and
  selectable.
- **Question details carry the action.** Opening a row shows the quiz, course,
  and question ID, the question text itself, and every compromised link with
  its source. Links are checkboxes, so a takedown can be issued for two of two,
  or all of them, without leaving the panel.

## Built twice

WebSweep was first designed on the legacy design system, the one with 587 card
variants and no semantic token layer. While it was in progress I was leading the
[overhaul of that system](/work/design-system-overhaul), and the overhaul
shipped in January 2026 before WebSweep did.

That left a choice: launch a brand-new product on a system we had just retired,
or redesign it on the new one and hold the launch window. I redesigned it. The
work ran from February, went to beta with real customers in April, and the first
tier launched in May 2026.

It was the first new product surface released on the overhauled system, which
made it the first real test of the design language. A system proves itself on a
product, not in a library.

## The design language, applied

The overhaul had set principles on paper: no product logic in components, no
variants for hypothetical use, start from the foundation and let the foundation
grow. WebSweep is where those principles met a product with its own needs.

Most of the dashboard composed cleanly from what the system already had. Two
things did not exist yet, and rather than build them as one-off product
components, both were designed as system components and fed back into the
library. That is the "let the foundation grow" principle doing its job: a real
pattern shows up in a real product, and the system absorbs it.

## Two components the system did not have

**A graph component.** The resolution-rate ring and the activity-over-time
chart were the first data visualizations the system had to carry. They were
designed on the semantic token layer, so chart color, typography, and spacing
come from the same tokens as everything else and the graph themes with the rest
of the product instead of carrying its own palette.

**A table that becomes cards.** The compromised content table is dense by
nature: course, quiz, content, link counts, status. It reads well on a wide
screen and falls apart on a phone. Rather than a separate mobile component, the
table is one component with two presentations: rows on desktop, and on mobile
each row collapses into a card that keeps the same data and the same actions.
One data model, one set of behaviours, no fork.
